Why beginners struggle with Hatha yoga —
and how to change that

Anastasiya June 2025 6 min read Yoga · Beginners

The most common mistake new students make in their first Hatha yoga session isn't a wrong pose. It isn't a misaligned warrior or a wobbly tree. It is something far simpler, and far more invisible: they hold their breath.

I've taught hundreds of first-time students, and within the first ten minutes of almost every introductory class, I notice the same thing. The moment a posture becomes slightly unfamiliar — the moment the body is asked to do something it hasn't done before — breathing stops. Shoulders creep toward ears. Jaws tighten. The whole body braces, as if the physical challenge were also a threat.

It isn't. And once students understand that, everything changes.

"Flexibility is not a prerequisite for yoga. It is a result of it. The only thing you need to bring to your first class is a willingness to breathe."

Why this happens — and why it's completely understandable

Our nervous system is wired to hold the breath under perceived stress. It's a survival reflex, ancient and automatic. When something is new, unfamiliar, or effortful, the body often interprets that as a signal to brace. This is not a character flaw. It is physiology.

The problem in yoga is that holding the breath creates the very tension we're trying to release. Without a steady inhale and exhale, muscles cannot soften. The parasympathetic nervous system — the one responsible for rest, recovery, and ease — never gets the signal that it's safe to switch on. The session becomes a gritting-of-teeth rather than a practice.

Hatha yoga, at its foundation, is a breathing practice that uses physical postures as its vehicle. Not the other way around. When students understand this, the entire orientation shifts.

✦

The three shifts that transform a first session

1. Breathe before you move

Before entering any posture, take two full breaths — a slow inhale through the nose, a long exhale through the nose or mouth. This is not a warm-up ritual. It is the practice itself beginning. The movement that follows will be softer, more open, and more sustainable because the nervous system has had a moment to register safety.

Try this in your next session
  • Before each new pose, pause and take one slow breath
  • If you notice you've stopped breathing mid-pose, soften — don't push harder
  • Let the exhale be slightly longer than the inhale to activate the rest response
  • If you can't breathe comfortably in a pose, you've gone too far

2. Use discomfort as information, not as a target

Western fitness culture often frames effort as virtue — the harder, the better. Yoga asks something different. In Hatha practice, mild sensation in a muscle (a gentle stretch, a slight warmth) is useful information. Sharp, pinching, or joint pain is a signal to come out. The goal is not to endure; it is to listen.

Many beginners push past the point of usefulness because they believe the pose "should" look a certain way. The pose should feel a certain way. And if it doesn't feel like ease with gentle effort — if it feels like force — then the body is telling you something worth hearing.

3. Release the idea of what you should look like

In an online or studio class, it is tempting to watch others and adjust yourself to match. Resist this as much as you can. Your body has a different history than every other body in the room. Years of desk work, old injuries, hypermobile joints, tight hips — these all shape what a pose looks like for you, and that variation is not a deviation. It is honesty.

A forward fold that keeps your knees generously bent and your spine long is a vastly better Hatha yoga pose than a straight-legged version that rounds your back and grips your hamstrings. What serves your body is correct. What mimics the picture does not always.

✦

A note for those who came back after a long break

Everything above applies doubly if you're returning to yoga after months or years away. The body remembers, but it also changes. The range you had before may not be there on the first day back, and that is entirely normal. Approach a returning practice with the same curiosity and gentleness you'd bring to a first session. The body rewards patience.

I often tell students: the yoga mat is one of the few places in modern life where doing less is sometimes the most courageous choice. Coming out of a pose early, resting in child's pose, simply lying down and breathing — these are not failures. They are the practice.

"The willingness to show up — to roll out the mat even when you don't feel ready — is the real definition of a yoga practice."

If you're a beginner, I hope this gives you permission to be exactly where you are. Your first session doesn't need to be graceful. It doesn't need to look like anything. It just needs to happen — breath by breath, one small shift at a time.

That is more than enough. That is, in fact, everything.
