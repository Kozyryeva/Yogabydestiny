The evening ritual that helped me
sleep through the night

Anastasiya February 2025 5 min read Lifestyle · Sleep · Yoga

For three years, I woke at 2 a.m. every night without fail. Not in a panic, not from a bad dream — just awake, mind quietly active, watching the dark ceiling until exhaustion eventually pulled me back under. By morning I felt wrung out. By afternoon, hollowed.

I tried everything the standard advice recommends: no screens after nine, cooler room, no caffeine after midday. These helped a little. What changed everything was twenty minutes of yoga and breathwork before bed — not to exhaust myself, but to signal to my nervous system that the day was genuinely over.

"Sleep isn't something we fall into. It's something the body is invited into — when it finally feels safe enough to let go."

Why the body struggles to switch off

The modern evening is, neurologically speaking, still daytime. Screens emit light that suppresses melatonin. Unresolved tasks and tomorrow's anxieties keep the sympathetic nervous system (our "do" system) mildly activated. We lie down, but the body hasn't received a clear signal that it's safe to rest. It stays on low alert — which is exactly why sleep is light, broken, or slow to arrive.

The evening practice I developed works by actively engaging the parasympathetic nervous system — the branch responsible for rest, digestion, and repair. It doesn't put you to sleep the way a sedative does. It removes the obstacles that were keeping you from it.

The twenty-minute evening sequence

Minutes 1–5 — Legs up the wall (Viparita Karani)

Sit sideways against a wall, then swing your legs up so they rest vertically. Let your arms fall open at your sides, palms facing up. Close your eyes. This gentle inversion reverses blood flow from the legs, calms the nervous system, and signals — perhaps more powerfully than anything else I know — that effort is over. Breathe naturally here. Five minutes feels like twenty.

Minutes 6–10 — Supine twist

Lie on your back. Hug both knees to your chest for a breath, then let them fall to one side while you open your top arm wide. Turn your gaze the opposite direction if that feels comfortable. Hold for four to five breaths, then switch. The twist gently massages the organs, releases the lower back, and continues the work of downregulating the nervous system that the previous pose began.

The sequence at a glance
  • 5 min — Legs up the wall, eyes closed, natural breath
  • 5 min — Supine twist, both sides
  • 5 min — Child's pose with extended arms
  • 5 min — Savasana with 4-6 breath (inhale 4, exhale 6)

Minutes 11–15 — Child's pose

From lying, come to hands and knees, then sit back toward your heels and fold forward, arms extended or resting alongside your body. This posture is deeply restorative. The gentle compression of the belly against the thighs stimulates the vagus nerve — the long nerve that runs from the brain through the torso and is central to the relaxation response. Rest your forehead on the mat or a folded blanket. Breathe into your back body.

Minutes 16–20 — Savasana with extended exhale

Lie flat on your back. Let your feet fall open. Place one hand on your belly. Breathe in for four counts, breathe out for six. This is the same ratio as the morning practice — but in the evening, it accumulates the work of the whole sequence, deepening the parasympathetic response with each cycle. After five minutes of this, I am almost always asleep within moments of reaching my bed.

✦

What changed — and what might change for you

Within two weeks of starting this practice consistently, I stopped waking at 2 a.m. Within a month, my sleep felt deeper and more continuous than it had in years. I am not a sleep researcher, and I can't promise the same timeline for every body. But I can say that the physiology is sound: slow movement, deep breathing, and stillness before bed measurably shift the nervous system toward the state in which sleep is not just possible, but natural.

You don't need to be a yoga practitioner to try this. You don't need a mat, though one helps. You need twenty minutes, a quiet space, and the willingness to treat the end of your day as something worth attending to — gently, deliberately, and without urgency.

Sleep is not a performance. It is a surrender. And the body surrenders most readily when it has been given permission to do so.
